# 1. From invariant cohomology to rational certificates

[Contents](#technical-information/introduction) · [Next: explicit constructions](#technical-information/explicit-constructions)

The geometric objective is to support the involution-invariant part of \(H^{4}\) of a smooth sextic fourfold on a divisor. The certificate equation translates this objective into an exact identity involving rational functions. Its advantage is that a proposed solution can be verified independently of the argument or numerical experiment that produced it. Its difficulty is concentrated in constructing the denominator.

## The hypersurface and its targets

Write \(Z=(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},Y_{0},Y_{1},Y_{2})\), let \(\tau (X,Y)=(X,-Y)\), and fix a homogeneous sextic \(F\) over a characteristic-zero field \(k\subset \mathbb C\) such that \(F(X,-Y)=F(X,Y)\) and \(X=V(F)\subset \mathbb P^{5}\) is smooth. A polynomial is **\(Y\)-even** or **\(Y\)-odd** according to the parity of its total degree in the three \(Y\) variables. Explicit records usually use \(k=\mathbb Q\) or a stated number field. Globally \(Y\)-even is the standing hypothesis; invariance under each individual coordinate sign change is a stronger condition used by particular constructions. The central target list uses integers \(q\in \{1,2\}\); several constructions extend to other positive \(q\) with the same conventions.

The next section defines the residue of a form of top degree with a pole of any order along \(X\). It proves that the residues of the forms with pole order at most \(q+1\) are exactly \(F^{4-q}H^{4}(X,\mathbb C)_{\mathrm{prim}}\), for \(0\le q\le 4\). Lower-pole numerators can be multiplied by \(F\) to put all forms over \(F^{q+1}\).

Let \(\Omega =\iota _\epsilon (dZ_{0}\wedge \cdots \wedge dZ_{5})\), where \(\epsilon =\sum _{i} Z_{i}\partial /\partial Z_{i}\) is the Euler field. Since \(\tau^{*}\Omega =-\Omega \), the Griffiths form

\[
\begin{gathered}
\omega _A = A\cdot \Omega /F^{q+1},       \operatorname{deg} A = 6\cdot q,
\end{gathered}
\]

has invariant residue when \(A\) is \(Y\)-odd. Its residue lies in \(F^{4-q}H^{4}(X,\mathbb C)_{\mathrm{prim}}\). In particular, \(q=1\) presents the invariant (3,1) classes; \(q=2\) presents the invariant Hodge-filtration level \(F^{2}\) and can also contain a contribution of type (3,1). The ambient class is the square of the hyperplane class and is already algebraic. There is no invariant (4,0) part, because \(\Omega \) changes sign. The desired generalized Hodge statement is

\[
\begin{gathered}
H^{4}(X,\mathbb Q)^\tau  \subset  N^{1}H^{4}(X,\mathbb Q).
\end{gathered}
\]

Here \(N^{1}\) denotes the rational subspace generated by classes vanishing off divisors. It is a Hodge substructure, by the Gysin description proved in Chapter 3. Solving all \(Y\)-odd targets for \(q=1,2\) puts the invariant \(F^{2}\) part in \(N^{1}\otimes \mathbb C\). Complex conjugation then puts its conjugate there too, and \(F^{2}\) plus its conjugate exhausts the invariant \(H^{4}\). Taking supports for a finite spanning set gives one finite union of divisors supporting everything. Monomials form a convenient input basis, but targets may be arbitrary \(Y\)-odd homogeneous polynomials.

There is no loss in seeking an ambient equation \(D\) for such a support. The Lefschetz theorem on Picard groups gives \(\operatorname{Pic}(X)=\mathbb Z[\mathcal O_X(1)]\), so an effective divisor has class \(\mathcal O_X(e)\) with \(e>0\). Its section lifts to a homogeneous polynomial \(D\) because \(H^{1}(\mathbb P^{5},\mathcal O(e-6))=0\) in the restriction sequence. The lift is not a multiple of \(F\). A \(\tau \)-stable support can be represented by the even polynomial \(D\cdot \tau (D)\), with repetitions allowed.

## Residues of forms with higher poles

In this section \(X\subset P=\mathbb P^{n}\) is a smooth hypersurface of degree \(d\) with equation \(F\), and \(n\ge 2\). Complexes of forms are unshifted. Forms are holomorphic; the lemma below and its proof hold verbatim for algebraic forms.

The sheaf \(\Omega ^{j}_P(\log X)\) consists of the forms \(\alpha \) such that \(\alpha \) and \(d\alpha \) have at most simple poles along \(X\). For a local equation \(f\) of \(X\), such a form is \(\alpha '\wedge df/f+\alpha ''\) with \(\alpha '\) and \(\alpha ''\) regular, and its residue is the restriction of \(\alpha '\) to \(X\). The residue commutes with \(d\) and gives the exact residue sequence

\[
0 \to  \Omega ^{j}_P \to  \Omega ^{j}_P(\log X) \to  \Omega ^{j-1}_X \to  0.
\]

A form of top degree with a pole of order \(q+1\) is logarithmic only when \(q=0\). Its residue is defined through the pole-order filtration. For \(0\le p\le n\), let \(P^{p}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(*X)\) be the complex with terms

\[
\begin{gathered}
P^{p}\Omega ^{j}_P(*X)=\Omega ^{j}_P((j-p+1)X) \;\text{for}\; j\ge p,\\
P^{p}\Omega ^{j}_P(*X)=0 \;\text{for}\; j<p.
\end{gathered}
\]

Differentiation raises the pole order by at most one, so this is a complex. It contains \(\sigma _{\ge p}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(\log X)\), the logarithmic complex truncated to degrees at least \(p\).

**Pole-order lemma.** The inclusion \(\sigma _{\ge p}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(\log X)\subset P^{p}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(*X)\) is a quasi-isomorphism.

*Proof.* For \(p=n\) both complexes are \(\Omega ^{n}_P(X)\) in degree \(n\). Let \(p<n\). The quotient of \(\sigma _{\ge p}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(\log X)\) by \(\sigma _{\ge p+1}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(\log X)\) is \(\Omega ^{p}_P(\log X)\) in degree \(p\). The quotient of \(P^{p}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(*X)\) by \(P^{p+1}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(*X)\) is

\[
\Omega ^{p}_P(X) \to  \Omega ^{p+1}_P(2X)/\Omega ^{p+1}_P(X) \to  \cdots  \to  \Omega ^{n}_P((n-p+1)X)/\Omega ^{n}_P((n-p)X).
\]

Its differential sends the class of \(\gamma /f^{m}\) to the class of \(-m\cdot df\wedge \gamma /f^{m+1}\). Up to these nonzero factors it is exterior multiplication by \(df\) on the restriction of \(\Omega ^{\bullet}_P\) to \(X\), which is exact because \(df\) vanishes nowhere on the smooth \(X\). The quotient is therefore exact above degree \(p\). In degree \(p\) its cohomology consists of the \(\gamma /f\) with \(df\wedge \gamma \) divisible by \(f\), and these are the sections of \(\Omega ^{p}_P(\log X)\). So the inclusion is a quasi-isomorphism on these quotients, and descending induction on \(p\) proves the lemma. ∎

This is the case of a smooth divisor in [Deligne, Théorie de Hodge II, Proposition 3.1.11](https://www.numdam.org/item/PMIHES_1971__40__5_0/).

**The residue.** Let \(0\le q\le n-1\), and let \(\omega \) be a section of \(\Omega ^{n}_P((q+1)X)\) over an open set \(W\subset P\). It is a cocycle of degree \(n\) in \(P^{n-q}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(*X)\). Its residue \(\operatorname{Res}_X(\omega )\) is the image of its class under

\[
\begin{gathered}
\mathbb H^{n}(W,P^{n-q}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(*X)) \cong  \mathbb H^{n}(W,\sigma _{\ge n-q}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(\log X))\\
\to  \mathbb H^{n-1}(X\cap W,\sigma _{\ge n-q-1}\Omega ^{\bullet}_X) \to  H^{n-1}(X\cap W,\mathbb C).
\end{gathered}
\]

The isomorphism is the inverse of the one given by the lemma. The second map is induced by the residue, and the third by the inclusion into the holomorphic de Rham complex, which resolves \(\mathbb C\).

The residue is compatible with restriction to a smaller open set, and it does not change when \(q\) is replaced by a larger integer. It does not change when \(d\beta \) is added to \(\omega \), for a section \(\beta \) of \(\Omega ^{n-1}_P(q\cdot X)\) over \(W\), because \(\beta \) is a cochain of the same complex. When \(\omega \) has a simple pole, it is the class of the Poincaré residue of \(\omega \); in particular it vanishes when \(\omega \) is regular. For \(W=P\) it lies in \(F^{n-1-q}H^{n-1}(X,\mathbb C)\), the image of \(\mathbb H^{n-1}(X,\sigma _{\ge n-1-q}\Omega ^{\bullet}_X)\). On Čech cochains, a representative is the total cocycle of [Diamond, Construction 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.29157v2#S2.SS1).

**Residue theorem.** For \(0\le q\le n-1\), the residues of the global sections of \(\Omega ^{n}_P((q+1)X)\) are exactly the classes in \(F^{n-1-q}H^{n-1}(X,\mathbb C)\) annihilated by the Gysin map to \(H^{n+1}(P,\mathbb C)\). These classes form \(F^{n-1-q}H^{n-1}(X,\mathbb C)_{\mathrm{prim}}\).

*Proof.* Bott vanishing gives \(H^{i}(P,\Omega ^{j}_P(b))=0\) for \(i>0\) and \(b>0\), as recalled in [Mustaţă–Popa, Hodge ideals, §25](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~mpopa/papers/SI.pdf). The terms of \(P^{n-q}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(*X)\) therefore have no higher cohomology. Its hypercohomology is the cohomology of its global sections, and every class of degree \(n\) is the class of a global section of \(\Omega ^{n}_P((q+1)X)\). By the lemma, the residues are the images in \(H^{n-1}(X,\mathbb C)\) of the classes in \(\mathbb H^{n}(P,\sigma _{\ge n-q}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(\log X))\). The residue sequences form an exact sequence of complexes

\[
0 \to  \sigma _{\ge n-q}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P \to  \sigma _{\ge n-q}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(\log X) \to  \sigma _{\ge n-q-1}\Omega ^{\bullet}_X[-1] \to  0.
\]

The image of the residue map in \(\mathbb H^{n-1}(X,\sigma _{\ge n-q-1}\Omega ^{\bullet}_X)\) is therefore the kernel of the connecting map to \(\mathbb H^{n+1}(P,\sigma _{\ge n-q}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P)\). Since \(H^{i}(P,\Omega ^{j}_P)=0\) for \(i\ne j\), the latter group injects into \(H^{n+1}(P,\mathbb C)\). So a class in \(F^{n-1-q}H^{n-1}(X,\mathbb C)\) is a residue exactly when the connecting map of the untruncated sequence annihilates it. That map is the Gysin map up to a nonzero factor, and for a hypersurface in projective space its kernel is the primitive cohomology; see [Voisin, Hodge Theory and Complex Algebraic Geometry II, §6.1.1 and Remark 6.8](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511615177). ∎

The theorem is due to Griffiths. [Diamond, Theorem 2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.29157v2#S2.SS1) proves on explicit Čech cocycles that the residues for all \(q\), together with the classes restricted from \(P\), span \(H^{n-1}(X,\mathbb C)\).

Let \(\Omega =\iota _\epsilon (dZ_{0}\wedge \cdots \wedge dZ_{n})\) for the Euler field \(\epsilon \). The global sections of \(\Omega ^{n}_P((q+1)X)\) are the forms \(A\cdot \Omega /F^{q+1}\) with \(A\) homogeneous of degree \(d\cdot(q+1)-(n+1)\). The residue of such a form lies in \(F^{n-q}H^{n-1}(X,\mathbb C)\) exactly when \(A\) belongs to the Jacobian ideal \(J(F)\) of \(S=\mathbb C[Z_{0},\ldots ,Z_{n}]\). Hence the Hodge-graded quotient \(F^{n-1-q}/F^{n-q}\) of the primitive cohomology is the piece of \(S/J(F)\) of degree \(d\cdot(q+1)-(n+1)\); see [Voisin, Theorem 6.10 and Corollary 6.12](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511615177).

## The key equation

For \(v=(v_{0},\ldots ,v_{5})\), set \(v(F)=\sum _{i}v_{i}\partial _{i}F\) and \(\operatorname{div}(v)=\sum _{i}\partial _{i}v_{i}\). A certificate for \((F,q,A)\) is a rational field with homogeneous components of degree \(6\cdot q-5\), with every denominator coprime to \(F\), satisfying

\[
\begin{gathered}
F^{q+1} \mid  K_A(v),       K_A(v) \coloneqq  A - q\cdot v(F) + F\cdot \operatorname{div}(v).
\end{gathered}
\]

Divisibility is in the local ring \(k[Z]_{(F)}\): clear the denominators, which are units there, and check polynomial divisibility. The field can be chosen with \(Y\)-odd components \(v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}\) and \(Y\)-even components \(v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\). Averaging with respect to \(\tau \) enforces these parities without changing the target.

The bridge to geometry is the rational four-form \(\beta =-\iota _v\Omega /F^q\). Homogeneity makes it a projective form: its weight is zero and contraction with \(\epsilon \) is zero. Its differential satisfies

\[
\begin{gathered}
\omega _A - d\beta  = K_A(v)\cdot\Omega /F^{q+1}.
\end{gathered}
\]

To prove the identity, put \(\mathrm{vol}=dZ_{0}\wedge \cdots \wedge dZ_{5}\). Cartan's formula and \(\operatorname{deg} v_{i}=6\cdot q-5\) give

\[
\begin{gathered}
d(\iota _v\Omega )=\operatorname{div}(v)\cdot\Omega -6\cdot q\iota _{v}\mathrm{vol},\\
dF\wedge \iota _v\Omega =v(F)\cdot\Omega -6\cdot F\iota _{v}\mathrm{vol}.
\end{gathered}
\]

Applying the quotient rule to \(\beta \) cancels the two terms containing \(\iota _{v}\mathrm{vol}\) and yields the displayed formula. The same calculation in \(\mathbb P^{n}\) for \(d=\operatorname{deg} F\) uses \(\operatorname{deg} A=d\cdot(q+1)-(n+1)\) and \(\operatorname{deg} v_{i}=d\cdot q-n\), and replaces \(6\cdot q\) by \(d\cdot q\).

The key equation therefore says that \(\omega _A-d\beta \) has no pole along \(X\). On the complement \(W\) of the denominator locus, \(\beta \) has a pole of order at most \(q\) along \(X\) and \(\omega _A-d\beta \) is regular, so the residue of \(\omega _A\) vanishes on \(X\cap W\). This proves sufficiency. The prescribed-support theorem below proves the converse. [Diamond, §2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.29157v2#S2.SS2) proves the corresponding equivalence on Čech cochains over a principal open set, with a holomorphic \(\beta \), and [§2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.29157v2#S2.SS3) makes \(\beta \) algebraic after shrinking that set. The bound on the pole order along the divisor is a separate statement, and it uses the Hodge filtration.

## One denominator and the normalized equations

Every finite rational tuple has a common denominator. We may choose it \(Y\)-even, multiplying by its involution conjugate when necessary, and write

\[
\begin{gathered}
v=N/D,       \operatorname{deg} N_{i}=\operatorname{deg}(D)+6\cdot q-5,       \operatorname{gcd}(D,F)=1.
\end{gathered}
\]

A common denominator introduces no restriction on certificate existence. The quotient rule gives

\[
K_A(N/D) = [D\cdot(A\cdot D-q\cdot N(F)) + F\cdot(D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N)-N(D))]/D^{2}.
\]

For this fixed \(D\), existence is equivalent to the following polynomial system:

\[
\begin{gathered}
E_{1}: q\cdot N(F)=A\cdot D,\\
E_{2}: D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N)-N(D) \in  (F^q).
\end{gathered}
\]

The forward implication is an Euler normalization, not an extra ansatz. Indeed, the key equation first forces \(A\cdot D-q\cdot N(F)=F\cdot H\) for a polynomial \(H\). Replacing \(N\) by \(N'=N+H\epsilon /(6\cdot q)\) makes \(E_{1}\) exact. Since \(\operatorname{deg} H=\operatorname{deg}(D)+6\cdot q-6\), Euler's identity also gives

\[
\begin{gathered}
D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N')-N'(D) = D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N)-N(D)+D\cdot H,
\end{gathered}
\]

which is precisely the expression already required to lie in \((F^q)\). Degrees and parities are preserved.

For fixed \(D\), both equations are linear in the coefficients of \(N\). One can compare coefficients after reducing \(E_{2}\) modulo \(F^q\), or introduce a quotient polynomial and impose an exact identity. Thus the unrestricted fixed-denominator question is finite linear algebra. Restricting \(N\) to a convenient smaller space changes that question and requires a separate completeness argument.

A certificate is **strict** when \(K_A(v)=0\) as a rational function. In normalized form this means \(E_{1}\) holds and \(E_{2}\) vanishes identically, rather than merely modulo \(F^q\). Strictness distinguishes individual witnesses. Ordinary and strict certificates have the same existence problem when the denominator is unrestricted, by the normal-form theorem in [§5.3](#technical-information/computation). That conversion may change the support and denominator degree, so the fixed-\(D\) system must retain the full \(E_2\) quotient.

## Euler freedom and denominator gauges

For every homogeneous rational \(\phi \) of degree \(6\cdot q-6\),

\[
\begin{gathered}
q(\phi\cdot\epsilon )(F)=6\cdot q\phi F=F\cdot \operatorname{div}(\phi\cdot\epsilon ).
\end{gathered}
\]

Consequently \(v\) and \(v+\phi\cdot\epsilon \) have the same \(K_A\). An Euler shift preserves strictness as well as ordinary solvability. With \(\phi  Y\)-odd it also preserves parity. The preceding normalization uses \(\phi =H/(6\cdot q\cdot D)\), whose poles remain legal.

There are also formally invisible Euler fields with poles on \(X\), such as \((P/F)\epsilon \) for \(\operatorname{deg} P=6\cdot q\). These are not legal certificate fields: the coprimality requirement forbids their denominator. A coefficient search which admits \(D\) proportional to \(F\) can therefore detect spurious solutions. Homogeneity and small residual alone do not certify legality.

One further gauge is useful for sextic denominators at \(q=1\):

\[
\begin{gathered}
(D,N) \mapsto  (D+t\cdot F, N+t\cdot A\cdot \epsilon /6).
\end{gathered}
\]

It preserves \(E_{1}\) and \(E_{2}\) modulo \(F\), and \(V(F,D+t\cdot F)=V(F,D)\). Coprimality is preserved as well. It need not preserve strictness, since it changes the rational field itself. This gauge is distinct from adding an Euler field to a fixed \(v\).

Indeed, put \(B_D(N)=D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N)-N(D)\). For \(q=1\) and \(\operatorname{deg} D=6\), the shifted numerator has \(N'(F)=A(D+t\cdot F)\). Euler's identity gives \(\operatorname{div}(A\cdot \epsilon /6)=2\cdot A\) and \((A\cdot \epsilon /6)(D)=A\cdot D\). Expanding then gives

\[
B_{D+t\cdot F}(N+t\cdot A\cdot \epsilon /6) = B_D(N)+t\cdot F(\operatorname{div} N+t\cdot A),
\]

using \(E_{1}\) to cancel the remaining terms. This proves precisely the stated modulo-\(F\) invariance.

## Prescribed support and pole multiplicity

The support of a divisor ignores multiplicities; a rational primitive does not. These facts must be kept separate when fixing a denominator.

**Prescribed-support theorem.** Let \(D\) be nonconstant, homogeneous, \(Y\)-even and coprime to \(F\), and let \(D_{\mathrm{red}}\) be a squarefree equation of \(V(D)\). For \(1\le q\le 4\), the following statements are equivalent:

1. \(\operatorname{Res}_X(\omega _A)\) vanishes on \(X\setminus V(D)\).
2. A certificate exists with poles confined to \(V(D)\).
3. A certificate exists with denominator \(D_{\mathrm{red}}^q\).

In particular, \(D^q\) also suffices. Over the field of definition of \(F,A,D\), the numerator can be chosen over that same field. The field can be chosen with the prescribed component parities. With the even presentation \(D^q\), its numerator has those same parities; a squarefree equation \(D_{\mathrm{red}}\) can itself be odd, in which case its numerator transforms accordingly. No smoothness or normal-crossing hypothesis is imposed on \(V(D)\) or its intersection with \(X\). Without a field-parity requirement, the same equivalence applies to arbitrary homogeneous \(D\).

We prove the slightly more general statement for a smooth degree-\(d\) hypersurface \(X\subset P=\mathbb P^{n}\), with \(n\ge 2, 1\le q\le n-1\) and \(\operatorname{deg} A=d\cdot(q+1)-(n+1)\). Put \(R=V(D_{\mathrm{red}}), e=\operatorname{deg} D_{\mathrm{red}}\), and work over \(\mathbb C\) until the final linear descent. We use unshifted de Rham complexes, with differential forms in degrees \(0,\ldots ,n\).

The two implications from support to certificates differ in nature. The second statement follows from the pole-order lemma on the affine complement of \(R\), without Hodge theory. The pole bound of the third statement uses the Hodge filtration.

**Forms and fields.** Let \(m\ge 0\). The identity \(\Omega ^{n-1}_P\cong T_P(-n-1)\) and the twisted Euler sequence give

\[
0 \to  \mathcal O_P(q\cdot d+m\cdot e-n-1) \to  \mathcal O_P(q\cdot d+m\cdot e-n)^{n+1} \to  \Omega ^{n-1}_P(q\cdot X+m\cdot R) \to  0.
\]

The left term has zero \(H^{1}\). Therefore every global section \(\beta \) of the right term is represented by a homogeneous polynomial tuple \(N\) of degree \(q\cdot d+m\cdot e-n\):

\[
\begin{gathered}
\beta =-\iota _N\Omega /(F^q\cdot D_{\mathrm{red}}^m).
\end{gathered}
\]

With \(v=N/D_{\mathrm{red}}^m\), the differential identity of the key equation shows that \(v\) is a certificate exactly when \(\omega _A-d\beta \) has no pole along \(X\).

**Proof of \(1\Longrightarrow 2\).** Put \(U=P\setminus R\) and \(X^{\circ}=X\cap U\). Both are smooth affine varieties. Taking sections over \(U\) is therefore exact on quasi-coherent sheaves, and the hypercohomology over \(U\) of a complex of such sheaves is the cohomology of its sections. All forms in this proof are algebraic.

By the pole-order lemma for algebraic forms, the cocycle \(\omega _A\) of \(\Gamma (U,P^{n-q}\Omega ^{\bullet}_P(*X))\) is cohomologous to a logarithmic one:

\[
\begin{gathered}
\omega _A=d\psi +\omega _{1},       \psi \in \Gamma (U,\Omega ^{n-1}_P(q\cdot X)),       \omega _{1}\in \Gamma (U,\Omega ^{n}_P(X)).
\end{gathered}
\]

No hypothesis has been used so far. Let \(\rho \in \Gamma (X^{\circ},\Omega ^{n-1}_X)\) be the Poincaré residue of \(\omega _{1}\). By the properties of the residue, \(\rho \) represents \(\operatorname{Res}_X(\omega _A)\) on \(X^{\circ}\). By [Grothendieck's comparison theorem](https://www.numdam.org/item/PMIHES_1966__29__95_0/), the cohomology of the smooth affine variety \(X^{\circ}\) is that of its complex of algebraic forms. In top degree this reads

\[
H^{n-1}(X^{\circ},\mathbb C)=\Gamma (X^{\circ},\Omega ^{n-1}_X)/d\Gamma (X^{\circ},\Omega ^{n-2}_X).
\]

The first statement therefore gives an algebraic \(\sigma \) with \(d\sigma =\rho \). The residue sequence

\[
0 \to  \Omega ^{n-1}_P \to  \Omega ^{n-1}_P(\log X) \to  \Omega ^{n-2}_X \to  0
\]

is exact on sections over \(U\), so \(\sigma \) is the residue of a logarithmic form \(\widetilde\sigma \) over \(U\). Residues commute with \(d\). Thus \(\omega _{1}-d\widetilde\sigma \) is logarithmic with zero residue, and so it is regular on \(U\).

Put \(\beta =\psi +\widetilde\sigma \). It has poles of order at most \(q\) along \(X\), and \(\omega _A-d\beta \) is regular on \(U\). A section over \(U\) is a rational section with poles along \(R\), so \(\beta \) is a global section of \(\Omega ^{n-1}_P(q\cdot X+m\cdot R)\) for some \(m\). The paragraph on forms and fields turns it into a certificate with denominator \(D_{\mathrm{red}}^m\). ∎

The argument follows [Diamond, Construction 2.14](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.29157v2#S2.SS3). There the exactness of \(\rho \) comes from a primitive of \(\omega _A\) modulo regular forms, on an open set where a partial derivative of \(F\) is invertible. Here it is the hypothesis, and affineness of \(U\) replaces the invertible derivative, so the support is not enlarged. The argument gives no bound on \(m\).

**Proof of \(1\Longrightarrow 3\).** The external inputs are the following standard facts about mixed Hodge modules and projective space. They are stated here to specify exactly what the proof uses.

- Localization along a reduced divisor \(T\) carries a Hodge filtration \(\mathcal F\) with \(\mathcal F_{-1}\mathcal O_P(*T)=0\) and \(\mathcal F_{k}\mathcal O_P(*T)\subset \mathcal O_P((k+1)T)\) for \(k\ge 0\); equality holds when \(T\) is smooth. Inclusions and quotients of these filtered modules are strict. See [Mustaţă–Popa, Hodge ideals, equation (0.1) and §§4–7](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~mpopa/papers/SI.pdf).
- Projective direct image is strict: for a filtered mixed Hodge module \(M\) on projective \(P\), the map from the hypercohomology of \(\mathcal F^p\operatorname{DR}(M)\) to that of \(\operatorname{DR}(M)\) is injective. Here its \(j\)-th term is \(\Omega ^{j}_P\otimes \mathcal F_{j-p}M\). See [Mustaţă–Popa, Hodge ideals for \(\mathbb Q\)-divisors, §1 and equation (1.1)](https://www.numdam.org/item/10.5802/jep.94.pdf).
- Algebraic de Rham comparison identifies localization with the derived direct image from the divisor complement; this is the comparison input used in [Diamond, §2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.29157v2#S2.SS3). Bott vanishing gives \(H^{i}(P,\Omega ^{j}_P(b))=0\) for \(b>0\) and \(i>0\), as recalled in [Hodge ideals, §25](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~mpopa/papers/SI.pdf); line bundles on \(\mathbb P^{n}\) also have zero \(H^{1}\) for \(n\ge 2\).

Set

\[
\begin{gathered}
M=\mathcal O_P(*(X+R))/\mathcal O_P(*R).
\end{gathered}
\]

The residue comparison is \(\mathbb H^{n}(P,\operatorname{DR}(M))\cong H^{n-1}(X\setminus R,\mathbb C)\), with the usual residue Tate shift. To see the shift, work first on \(P\setminus R\) with a local normal coordinate \(t\) for the smooth \(X\). Modulo regular forms, all higher normal poles are exact and the only normal de Rham class is \(\mathrm dt/t\), one degree higher than the corresponding class on \(X\). Localization along \(R\) extends this residue identification to \(P\). Since \(X\) is smooth, \(\omega _A\) belongs to the top term of \(\mathcal F^{n-q}\operatorname{DR}(M)\): it comes from \(\mathcal F_q\mathcal O_P(*X)=\mathcal O_P((q+1)X)\). If its residue vanishes, strictness of projective direct image says that its class already vanishes in \(\mathbb H^{n}(P,\mathcal F^{n-q}\operatorname{DR}(M))\). This is the only step that requires more than unfiltered de Rham exactness.

For \(n-q\le j\le n\) put \(a_j=j-n+q+1\) and introduce the finite complex

\[
\begin{gathered}
K^{j}=\Omega ^{j}_P\otimes [\mathcal O_P(a_j(X+R))/\mathcal O_P(a_jR)],\\
K^{j}=0 \;\text{for}\; j<n-q.
\end{gathered}
\]

Differentiation increases each pole order by at most one, so it gives the differential of \(K^{\bullet} \). The Hodge pole bound provides a map of complexes \(\mathcal F^{n-q}\operatorname{DR}(M)\to K^{\bullet} \). To check this on quotients, a filtered representative has poles at most \(a_j\) along both \(X\) and \(R\). A representative lying in \(\mathcal O_P(*R)\) therefore lies in \(\mathcal O_P(a_jR)\). Strictness identifies precisely those representatives with \(\mathcal F_{a_j-1}\mathcal O_P(*R)\), so the quotient map is well defined. Consequently the class of \(\omega _A\) is zero in \(\mathbb H^{n}(P,K^{\bullet} )\).

Each \(K^{j}\) has zero higher cohomology. Indeed, in

\[
\begin{gathered}
0 \to  \Omega ^{j}_P(a_jR) \to  \Omega ^{j}_P(a_j(X+R)) \to  K^{j} \to  0
\end{gathered}
\]

both twists are positive because \(a_j\ge 1\) and \(e>0\). Bott vanishing and the long exact sequence give the claim. Hence \(\mathbb H^{n}(P,K^{\bullet} )\) is computed by global sections. The zero class of the top section \(\omega _A\) supplies a global section \(\overline{\beta }\) of \(K^{n-1}\) with \(d\overline{\beta }=\omega _A\). The same sequence in degree \(n-1\), and \(H^{1}(P,\Omega ^{n-1}_P(q\cdot R))=0\), lift it to

\[
\begin{gathered}
\beta  \in  H^{0}(P,\Omega ^{n-1}_P(q\cdot X+q\cdot R)).
\end{gathered}
\]

Thus \(\omega _A-d\beta \) has poles only on \(R\). In particular, the primitive has at most \(q\)-fold poles along every component of \(R\) and along \(X\), with no added support.

The paragraph on forms and fields, with \(m=q\), turns \(\beta \) into a certificate with denominator \(D_{\mathrm{red}}^q\). This proves \(1\Longrightarrow 3\). The implication \(3\Longrightarrow 2\) is immediate, and \(2\Longrightarrow 1\) is the residue argument already given. Multiplying \(N\) by \((D/D_{\mathrm{red}})^q\) gives the prescribed denominator \(D^q\). Averaging the field under \(\tau \) preserves this even denominator and the target, and enforces the component parities. It can also retain \(D_{\mathrm{red}}^q\), since stability of its reduced support implies \(D_{\mathrm{red}}\circ \tau =\pm D_{\mathrm{red}}\). Finally, the fixed-denominator normalized equations form a finite linear system over the original coefficient field. Consistency over \(\mathbb C\) implies consistency over that field by Gaussian elimination. ∎

This also proves the claimed necessity of certificates for an arbitrary divisor support: choose an ambient \(D\) cutting that divisor, apply the theorem, and use the resulting polynomial tuple. If the original coefficients lie in a smaller field, the descent argument below gives a certificate over that field, possibly with a different support. The proof bounds poles on a chosen support; it supplies no bound on the degree \(e\) of the support itself.

At **\(q=1\)**, therefore, existence over \(D\) is equivalent to existence over any \(D^{m}\). An exact failure of the full fixed-\(D\) linear system excludes every higher power of that \(D\). At **\(q=2\)**, the definitive test on its support uses \(D^{2}\). Failure over \(D\) alone need not exclude support.

The distinction is sharp. Set

\[
\begin{gathered}
D=X_{0}^{6}+X_{1}^{6}+X_{2}^{6},       E=Y_{0}^{6}+Y_{1}^{6}+Y_{2}^{6},       F=D+E,\\
A=X_{0}^{3}\cdot X_{1}^{3}\cdot X_{2}^{3}\cdot Y_{0}\cdot Y_{1}\cdot Y_{2},       q=2.
\end{gathered}
\]

Writing \(\epsilon _X\) and \(\epsilon _Y\) for the two block Euler fields, the strict certificate is

\[
\begin{gathered}
v=A(F\epsilon _X-D\cdot \epsilon _Y)/(12\cdot D^{2}).
\end{gathered}
\]

For the numerator \(N_{2}=A(F\epsilon _X-D\cdot \epsilon _Y)/12\), direct blockwise differentiation gives

\[
\begin{gathered}
2\cdot N_{2}(F)=A\cdot D^{2},       \operatorname{div} N_{2}=A\cdot F,       N_{2}(D^{2})=A\cdot F\cdot D^{2}.
\end{gathered}
\]

These identities prove both normalized equations, with \(E_{2}\) identically zero. The field has degree seven and the required parities. Fermat smoothness is immediate from its partial derivatives, and \(D\) is coprime to \(F\) because \(D\) and \(E\) involve disjoint variables and have no common factor.

There is no numerator over \(D\). To prove this without an ansatz, normalize \(E_{1}\) and average a hypothetical numerator over the six independent sixth-root coordinate scalings, projecting onto the character of \(A\). Both \(F\) and \(D\) are invariant, so this operation preserves the fixed denominator and the two equations. The retained monomials in \(N_{i}\) have exponent vector congruent modulo six to \((3,3,3,1,1,1)+e_{i}\). These six residues are all less than six and already sum to the required numerator degree thirteen. Thus \(N_{i}=c_{i}AZ_{i}\) is the entire surviving space, not a selected family. \(E_{1}\) forces \(c_{0}=c_{1}=c_{2}=1/12\) and \(c_{3}=c_{4}=c_{5}=0\). For this numerator, \(\operatorname{div} N=A\) and \(N(D)=A\cdot D/2\), so \(E_{2}\) is \(A\cdot D/2\). It is not divisible even by \(F\). This excludes every original numerator because normalization and averaging preserve existence.

These bounds leave the central search problem untouched: they do not bound the degree of the reduced support that must be found.

## Combining certificates and descending coefficients

For fixed \(F\) and \(q\), the equation is linear in the pair \((A,v)\). If \(v_j\) serves \(A_j\), then \(\sum _{j}c_j\cdot v_j\) serves \(\sum _{j}c_jA_j\). A product of the denominators supplies a common denominator, so supported target spaces add when supports are united. A decomposition into symmetry characters can therefore organize a search without requiring one denominator to serve every component separately.

In cleared form, certificates \((D,N,Q)\) for \(A\) and \((E,M,R)\) for \(B\) combine as

\[
D_{A+B}=DE,\qquad N_{A+B}=EN+DM,\qquad
Q_{A+B}=E^2Q+D^2R.
\]

The resulting defect is \(E^2\mathcal E_A+D^2\mathcal E_B=F^{q+1}(E^2Q+D^2R)\). Nonzero quotient terms therefore cause no obstruction to addition. Denominator legality is preserved by products, and for positive \(q\) each numerator has degree \(\deg D+\deg E+6q-5\).

If \(F\) and \(A\) are rational and \(v\) is defined over a number field \(K\), normalized trace gives a rational certificate:

\[
\begin{gathered}
v_\mathbb Q = \operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(v)/[K:\mathbb Q].
\end{gathered}
\]

To prove descent, sum the conjugate fields over all embeddings of \(K\) into a normal closure. Differentiation commutes with each embedding, so each conjugate serves the same \(A\). Their average is Galois-invariant and hence rational. The product of the conjugate denominators is the norm, clears that average, and has degree \([K:\mathbb Q]\operatorname{deg} D\) before cancellation. Each factor is coprime to \(F\), so the norm remains legal. Homogeneity and parity are unchanged.

The same argument works for every finite extension \(K/k\) in characteristic zero. It also suffices to descend an initially complex certificate for data over \(k\). Fix its denominator degree and write the normalized equations in the finitely many coefficients of \(D,N\) and the \(E_{2}\) quotient. These are polynomial equations over \(k\). Legality is an open condition: at least one coefficient of the remainder of \(D\) modulo \(F\) is nonzero. Choose such a coefficient and invert it using one additional variable. A complex point of this finite polynomial system implies a point over an algebraic closure of \(k\), by the Nullstellensatz; its finitely many coordinates generate a finite extension. Trace then gives a certificate over \(k\). This proves descent of existence, without claiming to preserve an initially transcendental supporting equation.

For a bundled target \(A+cB\), with \(1,c\) linearly independent over \(\mathbb Q\), the nondegenerate trace pairing supplies \(p\in K\) satisfying \(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(p)=1\) and \(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(p\cdot c)=0\). Indeed, evaluation of that pairing on the independent vectors \(1,c\) defines a surjective linear map \(K\to \mathbb Q^{2}\). Applying trace to the certificate identity for \(p\cdot v\) shows that \(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(p\cdot v)\) serves \(A\). More generally a trace-dual basis extracts every rational component of an extension-field target. Trace over passive function fields appears in Chapter 2, where commutation with the relevant derivatives is proved for that setting.

For completeness, character averaging also has an explicit formula. Suppose a finite diagonal group acts by \(\gamma :Z_{i}\mapsto \gamma _{i}Z_{i}\), with \(F\circ \gamma =\mu _\gamma F\) and \(A\circ \gamma =\nu _\gamma A\). Define

\[
\begin{gathered}
(T_\gamma v)_{i}=\mu _\gamma (v_{i}\circ \gamma )/(\nu _\gamma \gamma _{i}).
\end{gathered}
\]

The chain rule gives \(K_A(T_\gamma v)=K_A(v)\circ \gamma /\nu _\gamma \). Thus averaging \(T_\gamma v\) preserves the certificate and projects onto the required character. A common denominator is the product of the conjugates, so its degree may grow by the group order. This does not justify imposing a single character on \(D\) at a previously chosen degree.

There is a useful exponent description when \(F\) contains all six pure sixth powers. Let \(L(F)\) be the lattice generated by differences of exponents in the support of \(F\). Diagonal substitutions preserving \(F\) up to scalar are exactly the characters of \(\mathbb Z^{6}/L(F)\). Modulo global scalar substitutions this is a finite group: the six pure powers force all \(\gamma _{i}^{6}\) to agree, and a common scalar makes them all one. Characters separate its exponent cosets at fixed degree. For a character field component, reduced numerator and denominator are both eigenvectors: the identity obtained by transforming their coprime quotient forces each factor to be carried to a scalar multiple of itself. Their characters differ by that of \(A\) times the \(i\)-th coordinate divided by the character of \(F\). This proves the support-coset rule without discarding rational fields, though averaging may increase degree.

For a full-support \(Y\)-even sextic, \(L(F)\) is exactly the lattice of vectors of total sum zero and even \(Y\)-sum. One inclusion follows from parity. Conversely, exponent differences realize all within-\(X\) and within-\(Y\) coordinate transfers, and also \(2(e_{X_{i}}-e_{Y_{j}})\); these generate that lattice. Hence all \(Y\)-odd targets of one degree belong to a single coset. Fine target-character decompositions are a property of structured \(F\), not an available simplification for general \(F\).

## Jacobian reduction and exact verification

Let \(J(F)=(\partial _{0}F,\ldots ,\partial _{5}F)\). If \(A=\sum _{i}G_{i}\partial _{i}F\), taking \(v=G/q\) gives

\[
\begin{gathered}
K_A(G/q)=F\cdot \operatorname{div}(G)/q.
\end{gathered}
\]

This lowers the pole order by one: modulo an exact form, \(\omega _A\) becomes \((\operatorname{div} G/q)\Omega /F^q\). Membership in \(J(F)\) therefore kills the leading Hodge-graded piece; it need not kill the whole residue class. For invariant \(q=1\) targets it does kill the class: the lower contribution would lie in the anti-invariant (4,0) space. Equivalently, after parity averaging, \(\operatorname{div}(G)\) is a \(Y\)-odd constant and hence zero.

For a concrete \(q=2\) example in our diagonal involution coordinates, take Fermat \(F\) and

\[
\begin{gathered}
A=6\cdot X_{0}^{6}\cdot X_{1}^{3}\cdot X_{2}^{2}\cdot Y_{0},       G_{0}=X_{0}\cdot X_{1}^{3}\cdot X_{2}^{2}\cdot Y_{0},       G_{i}=0 \;\text{for}\; i>0.
\end{gathered}
\]

Then \(A=\sum G_{i}\partial _{i}F\) is \(Y\)-odd and belongs to \(J(F)\), but one reduction gives the \(q=1\) numerator \(X_{1}^{3}\cdot X_{2}^{2}\cdot Y_{0}/2\). All of its exponents are at most four, so it is nonzero in the Fermat Jacobian ring \(\mathbb C[Z]/(Z_{0}^{5},\ldots ,Z_{5}^{5})\). Griffiths's graded isomorphism makes its (3,1) class nonzero. Thus the original \(q=2\) residue is nonzero, despite Jacobian membership. A complete reduction algorithm must retain this lower-pole target.

For an explicit field, clearing denominators reduces verification to polynomial identities and the homogeneity and coprimality conditions. A nonzero remainder divisible by \(F^{q+1}\) is valid. [Chapter 5](#technical-information/computation) gives the complete polynomial criterion and proves equivalence with strict existence when the denominator may change.
