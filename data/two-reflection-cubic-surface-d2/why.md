This sextic retains the individual reflections in \(X_0\) and \(Y_0\), but its other coordinates are coupled through terms such as \(X_1\cdot X_2\cdot Y_1\cdot Y_2\). It therefore lies beyond the literal “cubic in six squares” setting while preserving a much smaller piece of its symmetry.

Put \(u=X_0^2\), \(w=Y_0^2\), and
\[
B=X_1^2\cdot Y_1^2+X_1\cdot X_2\cdot Y_1\cdot Y_2+X_2^2\cdot Y_2^2.
\]
The terms involving the selected pair are \(u^3+w^3+5\cdot(u+w)\cdot(u\cdot w+B)\). The target
\[
A=X_1^2\cdot X_0\cdot Y_0\cdot(X_0^2-Y_0^2)
\]
is odd in both reflected coordinates. Its residue therefore belongs to the invariant sector of each of the two reflection quotients in [Chapter 3, §3.3](#technical-information/geometric-support).

The point of the example is to test how little symmetry that construction needs. The remaining couplings cannot be removed merely by choosing separate reflection coordinates in the \(X\) and \(Y\) blocks. A method depending on six individual sign symmetries would consequently be using more than the equation supplies.

The two-reflection sector contains 34 raw odd sextic monomials, or 32 directions after the elementary derivative relations. This short target gives a manageable entry into that larger sector without asking one formula to describe all of it at once.

This is a structured application of the cubic-surface viewpoint, with a particularly small selected target. It provides a baseline for distinguishing the existence theorem from the economy of a concrete formula. [The broken-reflection deformation](#case/quadric-contact-broken) keeps this target but perturbs the equation so that the selected reflections disappear. Comparing them asks exactly which part of the construction survives, rather than treating symmetry as an all-or-nothing label.
