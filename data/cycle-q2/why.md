Use the cyclic three-link sextic from [the degree-six cyclic instance](#case/cycle-three), with
\[
q=2,\qquad
A=X_0^2\cdot X_1^2\cdot X_2^3\cdot Y_0^2\cdot Y_1^2\cdot Y_2.
\]
Every coordinate occurs, and the unequal exponents in the final pair distinguish one position in the cycle. Cyclic permutation produces two companion targets on the same hypersurface. A construction for their invariant sum must still separate the other components to serve the specified target.

The pole order requires field components of degree seven and divisibility of \(A-2dF(v)+F\operatorname{div}(v)\) by \(F^3\). Multiplying a degree-six certificate by a polynomial contributes derivatives of that multiplier and does not generally give this identity. The [cyclic Jacobian construction](#case/cycle-three) establishes a different, second-order-pole identity.

There is also a representation-theoretic distinction. The sextic is invariant under the sixth-root diagonal action with weights \((2,1,0,3,2,1)\). The present numerator has weight seventeen, while the projective volume contributes nine, so the residue has character \(\zeta_6^{17+9}=\zeta_6^2\). Averaging to the invariant quotient therefore erases it. The [paired-sign sector theorem](#technical-information/known-locus/paired-signs-and-a-surface-quotient) supports the invariant sector; its conclusion does not cover this nontrivial character. This limits that quotient construction, rather than establishing nonexistence of a certificate.

The [holdout \(q=2\) target](#case/holdout-q2) belongs to the [cyclic-six family](#technical-information/known-locus/a-cyclic-six-family-and-complementary-quotients), whose deck/exchange support argument applies at every pole order. That theorem uses the separate action with weights \((0,0,0,5,1,5)\). The closing coupling here has weight four under that action and also breaks the auxiliary exchange. Thus the two sixth-root actions have different scopes: the one preserving this cycle supports its invariant residue sector, while the all-target cyclic-family theorem requires a different symmetry of the equation.
