The many coefficients in this sextic are organized around a conic rather than an additive separation of variable blocks. Introduce
\[
U=X_0^2-X_1^2,\quad W=X_2^2-Y_1^2,\quad
r=Y_0,\quad s=Y_2.
\]
With these quantities fixed, and \(x=X_1^2\), \(y=Y_1^2\), the equation is quadratic in \(x,y\). Its coefficients vary over the remaining parameters. This gives a small geometric object inside a six-variable problem without making the original hypersurface a sum of independent ternary sextics.

The target is
\[
X_0\cdot X_1\cdot X_2\cdot Y_1\cdot
(4\cdot X_0^2+5\cdot X_1^2-5\cdot Y_0^2).
\]
The product of four linear factors is the natural density of the quadratic-coordinate change; the remaining expression is affine in the conic coordinates. That match between the target and the auxiliary geometry is why this complicated-looking equation is a tractable test, rather than a random dense polynomial.

The entry retains the target of an earlier two-ternary example while changing its sextic by explicit cross-couplings. An exact Hessian calculation excludes every nontrivial linear direct-sum decomposition over \(\mathbb C\). Thus recognizing a useful conic cannot be dismissed as simply undoing a disguised linear separation.

The main question is whether the conic structure is enough to control a rational primitive and its divergence after transport. It also asks how the degeneration locus of a moving conic can organize the denominator, instead of selecting a large polynomial with no geometric guidance. The general transport and density principles appear in [Chapter 2, §2.5](#technical-information/explicit-constructions) and [Chapter 3, §3.4](#technical-information/geometric-support). This is one compatible target on a structured coupled family; it is especially useful for studying how constructions extend beyond visibly split equations.
